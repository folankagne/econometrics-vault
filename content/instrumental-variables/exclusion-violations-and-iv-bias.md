## Randomness and exclusion are separate conditions

A random instrument is independent of predetermined variables by construction — but that is not the same as satisfying the exclusion restriction. This distinction is exactly why the [generalized IV assumptions](../instrumental-variables/sutva-and-exclusion-restriction-for-late.md) separate "random assignment" from "exclusion restriction" rather than bundling them into one condition: a perfectly random instrument can still have a direct effect on the outcome, violating exclusion despite being randomly assigned.

## Why a mild violation can be worse than doing nothing

For $Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+u$ with $\text{Cov}(u,X)\gg 0$ (OLS badly biased), is it worth using an instrument $Z$ with $\text{Cov}(u,Z)>0$ but small (exclusion only mildly violated)? Comparing asymptotic biases:

$$\text{plim}(\hat\beta_{OLS}) = \beta_1 + \frac{\text{Cov}(u,X)}{\text{Var}(X)} \qquad\qquad \text{plim}(\hat\beta_{IV}) = \beta_1 + \frac{\text{Cov}(u,Z)}{\text{Cov}(X,Z)}$$

The IV bias is $\text{Cov}(u,Z)/\text{Cov}(X,Z)$ — even a *small* numerator can produce a *large* bias if the denominator (the first-stage strength) is also small. What matters is the **ratio**, not the numerator alone, so there is no guarantee that a "mildly" invalid instrument does less damage than a "badly" endogenous regressor; a weak first stage can amplify a small exclusion violation into a large asymptotic bias.

## The MSE case is even weaker for questionable instruments

Beyond bias, precision matters too: $\text{MSE}(\hat\beta) = [\text{Bias}(\hat\beta)]^2 + \mathbb{V}(\hat\beta)$. OLS is generally far more precise than IV (lower variance), so even when IV bias is somewhat smaller than OLS bias, IV's substantially larger variance can still leave it with the **larger** total MSE. This strengthens the case against reaching for a marginal instrument: a small bias reduction rarely compensates for a large loss of precision.

## The practical implication

Prefer instruments generated by genuinely **random events** — actual lotteries or random draws (e.g. Vietnam-era draft lottery by birth date, charter-school admission lotteries), or "natural" randomness with a clear, well-understood mechanism (quarter of birth and compulsory schooling, rainfall shocks, sibling sex composition) — over instruments whose exogeneity rests on a looser, harder-to-verify argument, precisely because the downside of getting exclusion even slightly wrong can dominate the upside of solving the original endogeneity problem.

This bias-amplification result is the formal version of a point Angrist and Pischke (2009, §4.1) make when discussing where good instruments come from: they favor instruments backed by direct institutional or experimental knowledge (draft lotteries, compulsory-schooling cutoffs) over ones whose validity rests on a subtler statistical argument, precisely because a first-stage that is strong and well-understood keeps $\text{Cov}(X,Z)$ safely away from zero — protecting against exactly the bias-amplification mechanism this entry derives.

*Source: Angrist & Pischke (2009), §4.1.*
