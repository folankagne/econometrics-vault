## The supply-and-demand example

The third classic source of endogeneity is **simultaneity**: when the outcome and a regressor are jointly determined by the same system, rather than one causing the other unidirectionally. The canonical illustration is a market with simultaneous demand and supply curves:

$$\text{Demand:}\quad y_i = -\alpha^d p_i + x_i^d b^d + u_i^d \qquad\qquad \text{Supply:}\quad y_i = \alpha^s p_i + x_i^s b^s + u_i^s$$

The quantity $y_i$ actually observed in the data is not generated by either curve alone — it is wherever the two curves intersect. Solving for the market-clearing price:

$$p_i = \frac{1}{\alpha_s + \alpha_d}\big(x_i^db^d - x_i^sb^s + u_i^d - u_i^s\big)$$

## Why this is endogeneity

The equilibrium price is a function of **both** noise terms, $u_i^d$ and $u_i^s$. Consequently:

$$\mathbb{E}(u_i^d p_i) \neq 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbb{E}(u_i^s p_i) \neq 0$$

Price is correlated with the noise in *both* equations simultaneously, because price and quantity are jointly determined by demand and supply shocks together — there is no sense in which price is "assigned" independently of the very shocks the researcher is trying to net out. Regressing quantity on price cannot recover either $\alpha^d$ or $\alpha^s$ in isolation: any observed price-quantity relationship is an unlabeled mixture of movements along the demand curve and movements along the supply curve.

## What identification requires

Disentangling the two curves requires a **shock that shifts one curve but not the other** — for instance, a cost shock that moves supply without directly affecting demand, which traces out points along the (stable) demand curve, or a demand shock (a change in consumer preferences or income) that leaves supply unaffected. This is precisely the logic that motivates [instrumental variables](../instrumental-variables/00-overview.md): an instrument for price would need to shift supply (or demand) without independently affecting the other side of the market, isolating movement along the curve of interest.

## Wooldridge's terminology, and a non-market example

Wooldridge (2016, Ch.16) formalizes this as a **simultaneous equations model (SEM)**: each endogenous variable's equation admits a *ceteris paribus* causal interpretation on its own, but the system as a whole must be estimated with this simultaneity in mind — plain OLS on any one equation is generally biased and inconsistent because each right-hand-side endogenous variable is correlated with that equation's own error term. The **order condition** for identifying a given equation requires that at least one **exogenous variable be excluded from it while appearing with a nonzero coefficient in the other equation(s)** — the SEM generalization of the exclusion restriction familiar from ordinary IV.

Simultaneity is not confined to literal supply-and-demand settings. Wooldridge's own example: estimating the effect of police force size on crime rates using panel data, $\Delta\log(crime_{it}) = \dots + \alpha_1\Delta\log(polpc_{it}) + \dots$, often finds a **positive**, counterintuitive coefficient on police per capita — not because more police cause more crime, but because city governments **simultaneously** expand police forces *in response to* anticipated or rising crime, exactly the reverse-causality structure of a two-equation system with police and crime jointly determined. Levitt (1996) addresses this using prison-overcrowding litigation as an instrument for prison population growth in a similar crime-equation SEM, finding a 2SLS estimate of the incarceration effect on crime ($\hat\alpha_1=-1.032$, se $.370$) substantially larger in magnitude, though far less precise, than the naive pooled-OLS estimate ($-.181$, se $.048$) — the same broad pattern (instrumenting moves the estimate, but at a real cost to precision) already familiar from the [statistical cost of non-compliance](../treatment-effects/the-statistical-cost-of-non-compliance.md) elsewhere in this vault.
