title: Time Series Methods — Overview
---
Both courses treat time as at most a source of panel structure ([panel data methods](../panel-data-methods/00-overview.md)) or a nuisance to be robustified against ([autocorrelation](../heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation/00-overview.md)). Neither develops time series regression as a subject in its own right: static and distributed-lag models, the time-series Gauss-Markov assumptions, weak dependence and the large-sample theory it underwrites, trends, seasonality, spurious regression, unit roots, and cointegration. This folder covers that ground from Wooldridge (2016), Ch.10–11, 18, and every entry is tagged `beyond-lectures`.

## Reading order

1. [Static and Finite Distributed Lag Models](static-and-finite-distributed-lag-models.md)
2. [The Time-Series Gauss-Markov Assumptions](time-series-gauss-markov-assumptions.md)
3. [Trends, Seasonality, and the Spurious Regression Problem](trends-seasonality-and-spurious-regression.md)
4. [Index Numbers and Event Studies in Time Series](index-numbers-and-event-studies.md)
5. [Weakly Dependent Time Series and Asymptotic OLS](weakly-dependent-time-series-and-asymptotic-ols.md)
6. [Unit Roots, Spurious Regression, and Cointegration](unit-roots-spurious-regression-and-cointegration.md)

See also: [autocorrelation and stationarity](../heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation/autocorrelation-and-stationarity.md), which already covers first/second-order stationarity and the random-walk non-stationarity example from the Econ 1 notes — this folder's Gauss-Markov assumptions (TS.1–TS.6) are the finite-sample counterpart to that entry's asymptotic stationarity requirements.
